Module 01 · 30 minutes

Grant readiness foundations

Before a reviewer reads a word of your story, someone checks whether you can legally receive and manage the award. Most first applications that fail are screened out here.

Who is the applicant?

Funders contract with a legal entity, not a chapter or a committee. Decide which of these will apply on your chapter's behalf before you write anything:

  • The chapter's affiliated 501(c)(3) organization or foundation with its own separate EIN and IRS determination letter.
  • A fiscal sponsor, an established nonprofit that receives the grant and manages funds for your program under a written agreement.
  • A partner applicant, such as a school district or college, with the chapter named as the program partner.

The chapter is not the 501(c)(3). An affiliated 501(c)(3) is a separate legal entity with its own board, bank account, books, and IRS filings. Grant funds go to that organization and are spent and reported under its controls.

STANDARD Confirm with fraternity leadership which entity may apply, who may sign, and how the fraternity name and marks may appear in an application.

Registrations for federal and many state awards

  • SAM.gov registration issues your Unique Entity ID (UEI). Federal awards require an active registration, and it must be renewed every year.
  • Grants.gov is where most federal applications are submitted. Roles inside the account control who can submit.
  • BEST PRACTICE Start registration weeks before a deadline. Validation delays are common and outside your control.

Your document library

Keep these in one shared, access controlled folder with a named owner and a review date. Check each item you have current.

How grant funds move

  1. The organization's board approves the application and budget.
  2. The organization's authorized officer signs and submits.
  3. Award funds go into the organization's account, never a chapter or personal account.
  4. The organization pays vendors directly or reimburses documented, preapproved program expenses.
  5. Chapter program leaders send receipts and program data to the organization on schedule.
  6. The organization reports to the funder and records the grant on its Form 990.

Keep the line clear

  • STANDARD A written memorandum of understanding (MOU) between the chapter and the organization, reviewed each program year. See the Template Kit.
  • STANDARD The organization's board authorizes every application by resolution or recorded vote.
  • STANDARD Charitable funds never pay membership dues, chapter social events, regalia, or anything that mainly benefits members.
  • STANDARD Members are reimbursed only for documented, preapproved program expenses under a written policy.
  • BEST PRACTICE The organization adopts a conflict of interest policy and records recusals in its minutes.
  • BEST PRACTICE Grant correspondence, letterhead, and signatures come from the organization, with the chapter named as the program partner.
Release gateNo application is submitted without the organization's board authorization and the signature of its authorized officer.

Write a data dictionary

Every metric needs one written definition that does not change between years. Two reviewers reading your numbers should reach the same count.

Undefined

"Active members: 40"

Active by what standard? Enrolled? Attended once? This number cannot be checked or compared year to year.

Defined

"Active Leaguer"

A student on the roster who attended at least 60 percent of scheduled sessions in the program year. Source: attendance log. Counted June 1. (The threshold is the chapter's choice; write it down and keep it.)

Collection standards

  • STANDARD One roster. Every student and mentor carries a unique ID used in every log.
  • STANDARD Record at the event, never from memory afterward.
  • BEST PRACTICE Reconcile attendance and rosters monthly; fix gaps while people still remember.
  • BEST PRACTICE Keep three years of history. Funders trust a three year trend over a single year.
  • OPTIONAL Move logs into a database such as the chapter's tracker so reports run themselves.

Protecting students

Kappa Leaguers are minors. Their data deserves the same care as their physical safety.

  • Consent. Collect signed parent or guardian consent for data use, for college outcome tracking, and separately for photos and stories.
  • Minimum collection. Collect only what you will use. Avoid storing sensitive identifiers you do not need.
  • Access. Store records in an access controlled system. Do not pass rosters around by personal email or text.
  • Aggregate reporting. Report counts and rates, never individual records.
  • Small group suppression. When a group is small, a percentage can identify a student. A common practice is to suppress results for any group under 10; set your chapter's threshold in writing.
  • School records. Grades and transcripts held by schools are covered by FERPA. Obtain them through the student and family with written consent or a formal agreement with the school.

Verify college outcomes

The National Student Clearinghouse receives enrollment and degree records from thousands of colleges. Its StudentTracker for Outreach service lets eligible outreach programs check where former participants enrolled, whether they persisted or transferred, and whether they earned a degree. Check eligibility and current pricing directly with the Clearinghouse.

  • BEST PRACTICE Submit each graduating cohort once a year and keep the returned file with your records.
  • STANDARD If you rely on student self report, label it "self reported" everywhere it appears.
  • STANDARD Include outcome tracking in the consent form families sign at enrollment.

Compare to a fair benchmark

A rate means little alone. Place it beside a reference group using the same definition, the same year, and the closest geography: your district's or state's college going rate from the state education agency, reports from the Clearinghouse Research Center, or federal statistics from NCES. Name the source and year in the sentence.

Say it honestlyStudents who join Kappa League and apply for scholarships may already be headed to college. That is called selection. Write "Of our 15 seniors, 14 enrolled" and "compared with the district rate," not "our program caused." Reviewers trust chapters that state their limits.

Module 06 · 40 minutes

Scholarship program compliance

Scholarships are a chapter's strongest evidence and its most regulated activity. The affiliated organization runs them under written procedures that a funder, an auditor, or the IRS could review.

Build noteTraining, not legal or tax advice. Have a tax adviser and counsel review your procedures and eligibility criteria.

Core requirements

  • STANDARD The affiliated 501(c)(3) awards and pays scholarships. The chapter never does.
  • STANDARD Written selection procedures adopted by the organization's board. See the Template Kit.
  • STANDARD Criteria and deadline are published and applied the same way to every applicant.
  • STANDARD A selection committee scores each application with a rubric and records the scores.
  • STANDARD Committee members sign a conflict of interest statement each cycle and recuse from any applicant they are related to or know personally.
  • STANDARD Awards are paid to the college for the student's account after enrollment is confirmed.
  • BEST PRACTICE Exclude relatives of the organization's directors, officers, and selection committee members, or follow your tax adviser's guidance.
  • BEST PRACTICE Keep the full file for each cycle: notice, applications, scores, minutes, award letters, payment records, and enrollment confirmation.

Private foundation or public charity?

The IRS treats these differently. A private foundation that makes grants to individuals for study generally needs IRS advance approval of its selection procedures under Internal Revenue Code section 4945(g). A public charity does not seek that approval, but it still must award scholarships for charitable purposes using objective criteria. Your organization's IRS determination letter states its classification. Confirm the rules that apply with your tax adviser.

Tax notes for recipients

Under Internal Revenue Code section 117, scholarship amounts a degree candidate uses for tuition, required fees, books, supplies, and equipment are generally excluded from income. Amounts used for room, board, or travel may be taxable to the student. The award letter should tell recipients to consult a tax adviser.

Eligibility criteria and the current legal climate

Scholarship and grant programs that limit eligibility by race have faced federal lawsuits under Section 1981 of the Civil Rights Act of 1866 and Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. Examples include a suit filed in March 2025 against the American Chemical Society's scholars program, a suit reported in April 2026 against the Congressional Black Caucus Foundation's spouses education scholarship, and the Fearless Fund case, which ended with the fund closing its grant program. Several of these cases settled before a final ruling.

  • STANDARD Counsel reviews every eligibility criterion before each cycle. Record the review date in the selection procedures.
  • STANDARD Selection uses only the written criteria.
  • BEST PRACTICE Review application terms too. Plaintiffs have argued that accounts, terms of use, and award agreements create contracts covered by Section 1981.

The law in this area is changing quickly. Check with counsel before every cycle.

Reporting rules

  1. STANDARD Report counts with numerators and denominators, never percentages alone.
  2. STANDARD Report group totals only. No student names or IDs leave the chapter.
  3. STANDARD State the verification method for each outcome metric.
  4. STANDARD Close the year on the same date each year, and have the organization's authorized officer attest the profile before it is shared.
  5. BEST PRACTICE When provincial or national leaders request Guide Right results, submit this same profile so every organization's figures line up. The National Guide Right Commission's member portal at kapsi-ngrc.org is one place such results could be gathered.

Pool the counts, never average the percentages

When results are combined, the combined rate is total enrolled divided by total graduates across all organizations. Averaging each chapter's percentage gives a chapter of 10 the same weight as a chapter of 50 and misstates the result.

Module 08 · 35 minutes

The community need statement

The need statement answers one question for the reviewer: why this program, here, now. It uses public data about the community, then your own data about the young men you serve.

Public data sources

  • U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey at data.census.gov: educational attainment, household income, poverty, and family composition by county, city, ZIP code, or school district.
  • State education agency report cards: graduation rates, college readiness, and college going rates by district and campus.
  • NCES: national education statistics and college data for comparison.
  • Local workforce boards and community foundations: regional needs assessments that funders already trust.

STANDARD Every number carries its source, year, and geography in the sentence or a footnote.

Four part structure

  1. The gap. One or two numbers that show the difference between where young men in your area are and where they could be.
  2. Who it affects locally. The schools, neighborhoods, and grade levels you serve.
  3. Why it persists. Barriers such as limited college guidance, cost, or few mentors with college experience. Cite where you can.
  4. Why you. The chapter's assets: trained mentors who are college graduates, a structured curriculum, and a program model the fraternity has run for generations.
Frame from strengthDescribe the young men as capable students who deserve access, not as problems to fix. Reviewers respond to dignity, and so do families who read your materials.
Need statement template
In [county or city], [percent] of adults age 25 and older hold a bachelor's degree, compared with [percent] statewide ([source], [year]). At [schools served], [percent] of graduates enrolled in college the following fall ([state report card], [year]). Students in these schools report limited access to [barrier], and [local evidence of barrier]. The [chapter name] Chapter serves [number] young men in grades [range] through Kappa League, pairing them with [number] trained mentors who are college graduates. [One sentence of your own outcome data.] This proposal will [what the grant makes possible] so that [outcome for students].

Program design and the logic model

A logic model shows the reviewer how your resources become activities and how activities become results. It keeps the narrative, budget, and evaluation telling the same story.

Outputs or outcomes?

Outputs count what you did

"Held 9 monthly sessions." "Logged 640 mentor hours." Necessary, but they do not show change.

Outcomes describe what changed

"Seniors completing the FAFSA rose from 11 of 17 to 16 of 18." Outcomes are what grant boards fund.

Write SMART objectives

Specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time bound. Each objective names who, how many, what change, by when, and how you will know.

Weak

"Help more seniors get ready for college."

Strong

"By March 31, 2027, at least 90 percent of Kappa League seniors will complete the FAFSA, confirmed by submission records."

Module 10 · 50 minutes

Writing the narrative

Reviewers read many proposals in a short window. Make yours easy to score: use their headings, answer in order, and put the evidence where they look for it.

Standard sections and what reviewers look for

Executive summary

Who you are, the need, what you will do, for how many students, what will change, and the amount requested. Write it last. Keep it to the length the funder sets, often a half page.

Organization background

The chapter's history with Guide Right, years operating Kappa League, students served, and two or three verified results. Lead with capacity to deliver, not with history alone.

Statement of need

Goals and objectives

Module 09. Two to four SMART objectives that match your evaluation plan line for line.

Program description and methods

What happens, how often, who leads it, where, and how students are recruited and retained. Include the mentor screening and youth protection practice.

Evaluation plan

Module 12. Each objective, its indicator, its data source, and when it is measured.

Sustainability

How the program continues after this grant: chapter dues allocation, other funders, partners, and volunteer capacity.

Budget and budget narrative

Module 11. Every line explained and tied to an activity.

Craft rules

  • STANDARD Answer every question the funder asks, in their order, under their headings.
  • STANDARD Every figure matches the data profile exactly, down to the denominator.
  • BEST PRACTICE Lead each section with its strongest sentence. One idea per paragraph.
  • BEST PRACTICE Use the funder's own words for their priorities so the reviewer can match you to the rubric.
  • BEST PRACTICE Pair one consented student story with the numbers. The story shows the person; the data shows it is not a lone case.
  • OPTIONAL One simple chart of a three year trend, if the funder allows attachments.

Using AI tools responsibly

AI writing tools can help outline, tighten language, and check a draft against the funder's questions. They also invent statistics, sources, and quotes that look real.

  • STANDARD A person checks every number, name, date, and citation against the original record.
  • STANDARD Never paste student names or records into a public AI tool.
  • STANDARD Follow any funder rule on AI use and disclose when asked.

Module 11 · 50 minutes

Budgets and the budget narrative

The budget is the narrative written in dollars. A reviewer should be able to find every activity from your program description on a budget line.

Key terms

TermMeaning
Direct costsCosts tied to the program: supplies, travel for college tours, curriculum, stipends, camp fees.
Indirect costsShared costs such as accounting, insurance, and administration that support the program but are not tied to one activity.
De minimis rateUnder the federal Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200) as revised in 2024, an organization without a negotiated indirect rate may charge up to 15 percent of modified total direct costs. Private funders set their own limits.
Modified total direct costs (MTDC)The base for that rate. It excludes items such as participant support costs, scholarships, equipment, rent, and the portion of each subaward above $50,000.
Participant support costsCosts paid for participants, not staff: stipends, travel, registration, and camp fees for students.
EquipmentUnder the revised Uniform Guidance, items costing $10,000 or more per unit with a useful life over one year.
In kindDonated goods, space, or volunteer time. Value it with a documented rate and keep the records.
MatchFunds or in kind the applicant contributes when a funder requires cost sharing. Only promise match you can document.
Scholarships in a grantSome funders will not pay scholarships directly; others fund only scholarships. Ask first. When scholarships are included, state the selection criteria, who decides, and how funds go to the college rather than to the student.

Module 13 · 60 minutes · Board members

Serving on your grant board

Each Guide Right foundation or affiliated 501(c)(3) has its own board or grants committee. That board approves the applications the organization sends to outside funders and reviews the proposals the organization receives. Fair, consistent decisions protect the organization, protect the chapter, and direct funds to programs that can show results.

Two jobs of the grant board

Approving outgoing applications

Confirms fit, budget, data, and commitments before the authorized officer signs an application to an outside funder. Uses the approval memo and board resolution in the Template Kit.

Board roles for reviewing proposals

Board or committee chair

Runs the cycle, assigns reviewers, enforces recusal, and leads calibration.

Program reviewers

Score need, design, and evaluation independently before any discussion.

Finance reviewer

Checks budget math, allowable costs, and the applicant's capacity to manage funds.

Data reviewer

Confirms metrics have definitions, denominators, sources, and protected student privacy.

Review process

  1. Intake screen. Eligibility, completeness, and required documents. Incomplete proposals get one chance to fix gaps by a set date.
  2. Conflict check. Each reviewer discloses conflicts before seeing proposals.
  3. Independent scoring. At least two reviewers score each proposal alone, with written comments for each criterion.
  4. Calibration meeting. Discuss any criterion where scores differ by two or more points. Reviewers may revise with a stated reason.
  5. Decision memo. Scores, decisions, and any conditions recorded and approved by the board.
  6. Feedback. Every applicant receives written comments, funded or not.
  7. Monitoring. Midyear progress report and final report against the approved objectives.
Release gate: conflict of interestA reviewer recuses from any proposal they helped write, any proposal they lead or will be paid from, and any proposal in which a relative or business partner has a role or would benefit. Recusals are recorded in the minutes.

Reviewer conduct

  • STANDARD Score what is on the page. Do not credit what you personally know about a program.
  • STANDARD Write one sentence of evidence for every score you give.
  • STANDARD Keep proposals and student data confidential.
  • BEST PRACTICE Separate polish from substance. A plainly written proposal with verified data can outscore a polished one with unverified claims.
  • BEST PRACTICE Offer coaching to promising proposals that fall short, so programs grow stronger each cycle.

Executive summary

Riverbend Guide Right Foundation requests $20,960 to run a full college pathway year for 24 young men in grades 9 through 12 through Kappa League. Trained mentors who are college graduates will lead monthly sessions, three FAFSA and application nights, two college tours, and summer leadership camp. For the Class of 2026, 15 of 18 program graduates enrolled in college the following fall, confirmed by enrollment records. This grant will help raise FAFSA completion to at least 90 percent of seniors and fall college enrollment to at least 80 percent of graduates.

Organization background

The Foundation is a 501(c)(3) organization that funds and administers the Guide Right program of the Riverbend Alumni Chapter. The Chapter has operated Kappa League since 2009. The Foundation holds all program funds, awards scholarships under board-adopted procedures, and reports to funders. Chapter members deliver the program under a memorandum of understanding renewed each August. Twenty mentors completed youth protection training and background checks this year.

Statement of need

In Riverbend County, 21 percent of adults age 25 and older hold a bachelor's degree, compared with 33 percent statewide (fictional county data). At the two high schools we serve, 58 percent of the Class of 2025 enrolled in college the following fall (fictional state report card). Counselors at both schools each serve over 400 students, leaving little time for one to one college guidance. Kappa League fills that gap with mentors who have completed college and can walk each student through applications and financial aid.

Results to date

For the Class of 2026, the Foundation awarded $18,000 in scholarships to 12 of 18 Kappa League seniors. With mentor support on applications, those students secured $142,000 in additional scholarships and grants, documented by award letters. Sixteen of 18 seniors completed the FAFSA. Fifteen of 18 graduates (83 percent) enrolled in college the following fall, confirmed by National Student Clearinghouse records, compared with 58 percent for their schools. Because students choose to join Kappa League, we describe these results as associated with participation.

Objectives

  1. By March 31, 2028, at least 90 percent of Kappa League seniors will complete the FAFSA, confirmed by submission records.
  2. By May 31, 2028, at least 85 percent of seniors will submit three or more college applications, confirmed by the senior tracker.
  3. At least 75 percent of enrolled Leaguers will meet the active participant definition (60 percent attendance) for the year.
  4. At least 80 percent of 2028 graduates will enroll in college the following fall, confirmed by an enrollment records match reported in January 2029.
